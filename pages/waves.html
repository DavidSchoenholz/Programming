How waves add up

Sound, light, the ripples in a pond and the hum of a power line are all waves. What makes them interesting is not one wave on its own, but what happens when several of them meet. Let's build that up from a single spinning point.

Imagine a point going round a circle at a steady speed. If you only watch how high it is, and plot that height over time, you get the familiar shape of a sine wave. The radius of the circle sets how tall the wave is, and the speed sets how tightly the wave is packed.

Now take two waves and simply add their heights together at every moment. When their peaks line up, the sum is twice as tall: constructive interference. Slide the phase of the second wave by half a turn and the peaks of one meet the valleys of the other. They cancel, and the black line goes flat.

Here is the surprising part. With enough sine waves of the right sizes and speeds you can build almost any shape at all, even one with sharp corners. A square wave is the sum of odd harmonics: a frequency of 1, plus a third as much at frequency 3, plus a fifth at 5, and so on. Each extra circle rides on the one before it.

That idea, that any signal is secretly a pile of spinning circles, is the Fourier transform. It is how your phone compresses music, how MRI scanners see inside you, and how the equaliser on a stereo knows what "bass" means.
